How Rail Status works

A status page for trains. When something breaks on a line, every passenger on an affected train hears about it within a minute, keeps hearing until it's over, and can ask questions.

The problem we solve

On 17 Sep 2026 at 17:22, an overhead line caught fire between Renens VD and Allaman, cutting the Lausanne – Genève line. The control centre knew within minutes and wrote about it constantly in its internal ticker. Passengers stuck on the trains heard almost nothing.

The big picture

Four boxes matter. The control centre produces events. The server turns them into incidents. An operator decides what passengers are told. Passengers' phones receive it.

The life of an incident

Like any status page, an incident moves through clear stages. Passengers never see the first one: it only exists so the operator knows something happened.

What the passenger sees

The app looks like SBB Mobile. We only change the places where SBB already talks about disruptions, so it feels like their app doing its job better.

From the SBB control centre

What the operator published: what happened, the official alternatives, and when the next update comes. This is the official word.

Our suggestion

What we work out from that plus the timetable: stay, wait, or change to a bus, and by when to decide. It's shown in grey and labelled as an estimate, never mixed with the official part.

There's one notification per incident, updated in place, and a chat that only answers from what was published. Its questions go back to the operator, grouped.

In the app today: check-in (the train's Bluetooth beacon after a one-time opt-in, or a QR code), live notifications that keep working with the screen off on Android, the incident sheet with the official update and our suggestion, and the chat.

Real and staged in the demo

  • Real: SBB's own data from 17 Sep (the internal ticker, event log and public messages), the timings in it, the AI drafts and chat answers, and the notifications on the phone.
  • Staged: the trains (four demo trains on the line, timed to "now"), the train's Bluetooth beacon (a stand-in; the QR code is the fallback), and the pace (the evening plays in 4 beats, one per keypress).

An automatic test clicks through all nine demo steps with a fake phone and a fake operator. It passes 9 of 9 against the live server.

Safety nets

  • No silence. If no operator publishes within 15 seconds in the demo (60 by default), the server tells passengers itself: there is a disruption, nothing more is known yet, and when the next update comes.
  • The AI only drafts. "Draft from the ticker (AI)" turns the internal ticker into an update in four languages. Staff names and phone numbers are removed before the text reaches the model. The operator checks every line and publishes. Without the AI, templates do the same job.
  • The chat sticks to what was published. Fixed rules answer most questions from the published updates. When they can't, an AI answer is used only if it passes a check against the published facts and contains no personal data; otherwise the passenger gets a fixed, safe reply.
  • Self-healing demo. The server and tunnel run as services that restart on a crash and survive a reboot. A watchdog checks the public address every minute.
  • Admin key. The dashboard and admin routes can require a key. It's off by default for the demo.
  • Real routes, optional. With a key for SBB's journey planner (OJP), the next move uses real trips around any closure. Without it, a hand-checked set of routes around Allaman – Renens VD is used.
